# Debt Signals | National Debt Statistics Investigation

## Why I chose this topic

I chose national debt because I want this project to show my growing interest in statistics, economics, and quantitative finance.

My curiosity was also influenced by the musical *Hamilton*. The story introduced me to Alexander Hamilton's role in developing the early United States financial system, especially his ideas about public credit, government debt, and the importance of building financial confidence. This made me ask: **how could debt encourage economic development, and how could it help protect the new United States at that time?**

I became particularly interested in Hamilton's argument that well-managed public debt could restore confidence in the government, attract investors, support trade, and give the country access to borrowing during emergencies. At the same time, debt created interest costs, political disagreements, and financial risks. I want to examine both sides rather than assume that debt is automatically beneficial.

This project is my first attempt to explore that curiosity using statistical methods. I am using data to examine the typical level of debt, its variation over time, and possible relationships with economic growth. The current data is illustrative, but I plan to develop the project using official fiscal and economic data.

This topic is meaningful to me because it connects a personal interest with a practical quantitative question: how can statistics help us understand the financial decisions made by governments?

## Historical inspiration: Hamilton and public finance

The musical also encouraged me to look beyond the story and research Alexander Hamilton's real financial work. In **“Cabinet Battle #1,”** the disagreement over whether the federal government should assume state debts introduces the political difficulty of managing national debt. **“The Room Where It Happens”** connects financial policy with negotiation and political compromise, while **“Non-Stop”** reflects Hamilton's extensive writing and influence in building the early financial system.

These references led me to read about Hamilton's **First Report on the Public Credit**, his proposal for federal assumption of state debts, and the creation of a national bank. The musical dramatizes historical events, but it gave me a starting point for asking how governments build public credit, encourage economic activity, and manage financial risk. My website develops this question through statistical analysis of national-debt data.
